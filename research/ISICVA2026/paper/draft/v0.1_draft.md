## Abstract

Electric wheelchair users, particularly children, face significant risk of obstacle collisions due to limited spatial awareness and slower reaction times. Existing commercial safety solutions cost between $1,850 and $4,040, are rear-facing only, and lack wireless connectivity. We present CAREC (Collision Avoidance with Real-time Edge Computing), an open-source, non-invasive obstacle detection system built on the SenseCAP Watcher W1-A — a single $59.99 device integrating a 5MP camera, Himax HX6538 neural processing unit, colour display, speaker, and Bluetooth 5. CAREC uses on-device NPU inference to classify obstacles into three distance zones, triggering synchronised audio, visual, and BLE caregiver alerts with end-to-end latency under 200ms. An optical-flow motion gate eliminates false positives when stationary. The complete system costs under $100, requires no wheelchair modification, and achieved [XX]% detection accuracy across 50 obstacle scenarios.

**Keywords:** edge AI, obstacle detection, wheelchair safety, NPU inference, assistive technology, computer vision

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Commercial Wheelchair Safety Systems

The primary commercially available safety aid for power wheelchair users is the Braze Mobility Blind Spot Sensor system, which integrates multiple ultrasonic proximity sensors mounted to the rear of the wheelchair frame [1]. The system retail price ranges from approximately $1,850 CAD for a basic two-sensor configuration to over $4,040 for extended coverage variants. While the system has been validated in occupational therapy settings, it carries three structural limitations relevant to CAREC. First, all sensors are rear-facing: frontal and lateral obstacles — the most common collision site for children navigating indoor environments — remain undetected. Second, alerts are delivered exclusively through haptic feedback on the joystick housing and an audible tone at the chair, with no wireless connectivity to a supervising caregiver. Third, installation requires drilling into the wheelchair frame, voiding manufacturer warranties and introducing a permanent modification that many families are unwilling to accept.

Semi-autonomous systems such as the Permobil SmartDrive and the Ottobock C1000 DS address adult power-chair navigation but are not designed for pediatric populations and carry system prices exceeding $8,000. Retrofit AI navigation accessories based on depth cameras (Intel RealSense D435) exist as research demonstrators but have not achieved commercial availability.

Wheelchair collisions are common: a community study of active wheelchair users found that 55% experienced at least one collision and 17% experienced two or more within a three-year period [14, 15]. Forward obstacles — walls, doorframes, furniture — are among the most frequent collision sites, yet no commercial safety system addresses them.

The result is a market gap: no commercially available, affordable, forward-facing, and wirelessly connected obstacle detection system exists for pediatric electric wheelchair users.

### 2.2 AI-Assisted Wheelchair Navigation Research

Academic research has explored machine-learning approaches to wheelchair obstacle avoidance across a range of hardware platforms. Deep learning approaches have demonstrated that RGB camera streams are sufficient for reliable obstacle classification without depth sensors. Tawil and Hafez [10] demonstrated a MobileNetV2 SSD detector running on Raspberry Pi 4 for powered wheelchair obstacle avoidance. Patthanajitsilp et al. [11] applied YOLOv3 to detect doors, pillars, and wall edges via a smartphone-mounted camera, achieving 80–90% accuracy. Giuffrida et al. [12] used U-Net semantic segmentation on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano for outdoor wheelchair navigation. While these systems achieve competitive accuracy, they share common deployment barriers: Raspberry Pi 4 and Jetson Nano platforms cost $150–$350, draw 5–15 W, and require custom circuit assembly and software integration beyond the capability of non-technical caregivers.

IoT-integrated wheelchair systems have been proposed that combine health monitoring, obstacle detection, and navigation assistance on a single embedded platform [13]. These prototypes demonstrate that multi-modal sensor fusion can improve detection reliability but typically rely on cloud connectivity for model inference, introducing privacy concerns and a hard dependency on network availability — both unacceptable for a safety-critical child mobility aid.

CAREC differs from prior academic systems in three key ways: (1) all inference runs on-device on a purpose-built NPU with no cloud dependency; (2) the entire system is contained within a single $59.99 commodity module requiring no custom circuit assembly; and (3) the system is designed for non-technical deployment, with a caregiver app, OTA firmware updates, and a non-invasive mounting system.

### 2.3 Edge AI for Embedded Vision Systems

The emergence of purpose-built neural processing units for microcontroller-class devices has enabled convolutional neural network inference at milliwatt power budgets. Chen et al. [16] surveyed NPU accelerator architectures, showing that dedicated multiply-accumulate arrays in devices such as the ARM Ethos-U55 achieve 10–100× energy efficiency versus CPU-only inference on the same silicon. The TinyML movement, surveyed by Warden and Situnayake [17], demonstrated that MobileNet-class models can run inference at 10–30 FPS on microcontrollers with as little as 512 KB SRAM when quantised to INT8.

The Himax WiseEye2 HX6538, used in the SenseCAP Watcher W1-A, integrates an ARM Cortex-M55 processor with an Ethos-U55 NPU within a single low-power SoC [3]. In always-on mode the device consumes under 1 mA while executing a continuous object detection loop. This enables embedded vision systems that operate for days on a small lithium polymer battery — a fundamental requirement for a wheelchair accessory that must be available for an entire school day without recharging.

Bounding-box-based distance estimation from a monocular camera is a well-studied problem in mobile robotics. When the physical size of the target object is approximately known, the apparent angular width of the bounding box encodes distance through the pinhole camera model [5]. This approach is less precise than stereo depth or time-of-flight ranging but requires no additional hardware and has been validated for indoor obstacle detection at distances of 0.3–2.0 m with errors of ±10–15 cm — sufficient for a three-zone alert system where the zone boundaries are 60 cm and 100 cm.

## 3. System Architecture

### 3.1 Hardware Platform

The SenseCAP Watcher W1-A Clear Enclosure (Seeed Studio, SKU 113991315, $59.99) serves as the computational and sensing platform for CAREC. The device integrates an ESP32-S3 dual-core processor (240 MHz, 8 MB PSRAM), a Himax WiseEye2 HX6538 AI accelerator (ARM Cortex-M55 + Ethos-U55 NPU), a 5MP OV5647 camera module (120° field of view, fixed focus at 3m), a 1.45" touch display (412×412 pixels), a built-in 1W speaker, and a microphone — all within a transparent enclosure weighing approximately 100g. Wireless connectivity is provided via IEEE 802.11 b/g/n WiFi (2.4 GHz) and Bluetooth 5.

The device is mounted to the wheelchair armrest using a standard 1/4" ball joint (360° rotation, ±80° tilt) and a 1–1.5" stainless steel tube clamp, requiring no drilling or permanent modification to the wheelchair. Power is supplied via USB-C from a 10,000 mAh lithium polymer battery providing over 50 hours of continuous operation.

**Total system cost:** ~$93–112 including mounting hardware and battery.

### 3.2 Software Architecture

CAREC firmware is structured as a four-layer stack (Fig. 2). At the base, Espressif's ESP-IDF runtime and the Seeed SenseCraft SDK provide hardware abstraction for the ESP32-S3 and HX6538 NPU respectively. Above this, seven application modules implement the detection and alerting logic:

- `sensecraft_detection` — initialises the NPU and requests inference results each loop iteration
- `distance_estimator` — converts bounding-box coordinates to a centimetre distance estimate
- `motion_detector` — classifies wheelchair motion state using sparse optical flow
- `display_alert` — drives the 412×412 IPS display with zone-colour fills and blink patterns
- `directional_beep_patterns` — generates non-blocking PWM audio alert sequences
- `ble_logger` — broadcasts GATT notify events to the SenseCraft Mate caregiver app
- `wifi_ota` — polls an OTA manifest and applies firmware updates when safe to do so

The top layer, `CAREC_obstacle_detection.ino`, implements a single 100 ms safety loop that calls each module in strict order: motion gate → inference → distance → zone → alerts → OTA check. Configuration parameters are centralised in three header files (`zone_config.h`, `wifi_config.h`, `build_flags.h`) with compile-time feature toggles so each module can be individually disabled without modifying application logic.

### 3.3 Detection Pipeline

Figure 3 shows the end-to-end detection pipeline. Each loop iteration proceeds as follows:

1. **Motion gate** (≈5 ms): The ESP32-S3 resizes the latest camera frame to an 80×60 grayscale thumbnail and applies Lucas-Kanade sparse optical flow tracking across 12 Shi-Tomasi corner features [4]. If the mean feature displacement falls below a stationary threshold, the wheelchair is classified as `MOTION_STATIONARY` and the loop returns immediately after setting the display to solid green — no NPU inference is requested, conserving power and eliminating stationary false positives.

2. **NPU inference** (≈80–150 ms): When the wheelchair is moving, the SenseCraft SDK submits the full-resolution frame to the Himax HX6538 Ethos-U55 NPU for object detection. The NPU returns a list of bounding boxes with class labels and confidence scores from a pre-trained MobileNet-SSD INT8 model.

3. **Distance estimation** (≈1 ms): `nearest_obstacle_cm()` iterates the bounding-box list, selects the box with the largest normalised width (closest obstacle), and applies the monocular distance heuristic described in Section 4.1.

4. **Zone classification** (< 1 ms): The estimated distance is thresholded against `DIST_RED` (60 cm) and `DIST_YELLOW` (100 cm) to assign one of three zones: `ZONE_RED`, `ZONE_YELLOW`, or `ZONE_GREEN`.

5. **Multi-modal alert output** (< 1 ms dispatch): `beep_trigger()` and `display_set_zone()` set the target state for the non-blocking state machines, which advance one step per loop iteration. `ble_log_event()` serialises a JSON payload and queues it for BLE GATT notification.

6. **OTA safety check** (< 1 ms): `ota_set_safe()` is called with `true` only when `zone == ZONE_GREEN`, ensuring a firmware reboot can only occur when no obstacle threat is active.

Total pipeline latency from frame capture to first alert output is under 200 ms in all tested conditions.

### 3.4 Alert Zone Design

Table 1 defines the three alert zones used by CAREC.

| Zone | Distance Threshold | Display | Audio Pattern | BLE Event |
|------|-------------------|---------|--------------|-----------|
| GREEN | > 100 cm | Solid green | Silent | None |
| YELLOW | 60–100 cm | Slow blink (600ms period) | Single beep, 1 Hz | Warning JSON |
| RED | < 60 cm | Fast blink (150ms period) | Triple burst, repeating | Critical JSON |

The 60 cm RED threshold was selected based on the typical stopping distance of a pediatric electric wheelchair at maximum speed (~0.5 m/s): a 60 cm margin provides approximately 1.2 seconds of warning time, sufficient for a child to release the joystick.

## 4. Implementation

### 4.1 Distance Estimation

The OV5647 camera provides a single RGB stream with no depth channel. CAREC estimates obstacle distance using a monocular bounding-box width heuristic derived from the pinhole camera model. For a reference object of known physical width *W* (cm) appearing at normalised bounding-box width *b* (range 0–1) in a camera with half-field-of-view angle *θ*, the distance *d* is:

```
d = W / (b × 2 × tan(θ))
```

For the OV5647 120° lens, *θ* = 60°, giving `tan(θ) = √3 ≈ 1.732`. With the default reference width *W* = 30 cm (representing a chair leg, doorpost, or human torso):

```
distance_cm = 30 / (bbox_w_norm × 2 × 1.732)
            = 8.66 / bbox_w_norm
```

The function `nearest_obstacle_cm()` selects the bounding box with the greatest `bbox_w_norm` among all detections returned by the NPU (the widest apparent object is closest) and applies this formula. The formula is evaluated in floating point on the ESP32-S3 CPU; the computation time is under 0.1 ms.

The two zone-boundary distances (60 cm and 100 cm) correspond to theoretical bounding-box widths of 0.241 and 0.144 respectively, which are stored as calibration constants in `zone_config.h`. These constants serve as unit tests: a calibration run places a 30 cm reference panel at each boundary distance and verifies that the live `bbox_w_norm` reading matches the stored constant within ±0.010. The calibration tool `tools/calibration/calibrate_distance.py` automates this by reading UART log lines matching the pattern `[BBOX].*w=([\d.]+)` and collecting 10 samples at each distance.

The primary limitation of this heuristic is object-width variability: a thin vertical obstacle (e.g., a chair leg viewed edge-on) produces a narrower bounding box than expected, causing distance to be overestimated. CAREC mitigates this by (a) using the nearest detection across all visible detections, (b) configuring `DIST_RED` and `DIST_YELLOW` with conservative margins, and (c) planning a Phase 2 ultrasonic rear sensor for environments with predominately thin obstacles.

### 4.2 Optical-Flow Motion Gate

A persistent problem with camera-based obstacle detectors is that a stationary wheelchair surrounded by normal indoor objects generates spurious detections whenever the object-detection confidence threshold is crossed. In our informal tests with the SenseCraft built-in detector, approximately one false positive per 40–60 seconds occurred at rest due to scene texture variability and model uncertainty. Over an 8-hour school day this would generate hundreds of false alerts, rapidly eroding caregiver trust.

CAREC addresses this with a pure-software optical-flow motion gate. At the start of each loop iteration, the ESP32-S3 CPU:

1. Downscales the latest camera frame to an 80×60 grayscale thumbnail (≈ 4800 pixels).
2. Applies the Shi-Tomasi corner detector to select up to 12 feature points from the previous frame that have sufficient image gradient for reliable tracking.
3. Runs Lucas-Kanade sparse optical flow [4] to estimate each feature's displacement vector between the previous and current thumbnail.
4. Computes the mean displacement magnitude and the net horizontal flow direction.

Three states are produced:

- `MOTION_STATIONARY` — mean displacement < `FLOW_STATIONARY_THRESHOLD` (default: 1.5 px at 80×60). The loop returns immediately: display shows solid green, all audio is silent, OTA updates are permitted. NPU inference is not requested.
- `MOTION_FORWARD` — net positive mean flow (scene expands, wheelchair moving toward obstacles). Full detection pipeline executes.
- `MOTION_BACKWARD` — net negative mean flow (scene contracts). Full detection pipeline executes.

This gate adds approximately 5 ms to loop latency and requires no additional hardware. In a 30-minute stationary test, zero false positives were recorded with the gate enabled, compared to [XX] false positives with the gate disabled (to be updated from Week 3 testing).

### 4.3 Non-Blocking Alert State Machines

Early firmware prototypes used `delay()` calls to implement beep patterns (e.g., 80 ms on, 40 ms off). This blocked the safety loop for the duration of the delay, preventing detection updates during an alert. With a 200 ms max latency budget, a 3-pulse burst pattern (3 × 80 ms + 3 × 40 ms = 360 ms) would cause the detection pipeline to stall for nearly two loop iterations.

CAREC replaces all blocking delays with two cooperative state machines, each advancing exactly one step per 100 ms loop iteration:

**Beep state machine** (`directional_beep_patterns.h`):
- `beep_trigger(pattern)` — sets a target pattern (`BEEP_NONE`, `BEEP_WARNING`, `BEEP_CRITICAL`) and resets the state index.
- `beep_update()` — called once per loop; advances the state index and drives the speaker PWM GPIO accordingly. For `BEEP_CRITICAL`, the sequence is: pulse 1 on (80 ms) → pulse 1 off (40 ms) → pulse 2 on → pulse 2 off → pulse 3 on → pulse 3 off → rest (60 ms) → repeat.

**Display state machine** (`display_alert.h`):
- `display_set_zone(zone)` — sets the target zone.
- `display_update()` — advances blink state. RED zone: toggle every 150 ms (fast blink, 3.3 Hz). YELLOW zone: toggle every 600 ms (slow blink, 0.8 Hz). GREEN zone: solid colour.

Both state machines track elapsed time against their timing constants without sleeping, keeping the main loop non-blocking. No RTOS is required: the 100 ms loop period provides the time base, and the state machines advance by the correct number of steps based on elapsed wall-clock time. This design also means that a zone change mid-pattern immediately overrides the pattern on the next loop iteration, ensuring alert escalation (GREEN→YELLOW→RED) has sub-100 ms response.

### 4.4 OTA Safety Gate

The SenseCAP Watcher W1-A supports over-the-air firmware updates via WiFi, enabling bug fixes and feature additions without requiring a USB-C cable. However, an OTA reboot during an active obstacle alert would silence all warnings at the exact moment they are most needed.

CAREC implements a safety gate through the `ota_set_safe(bool)` function:

```cpp
// In wifi_ota.h
static bool _ota_safe = false;

void ota_set_safe(bool safe) {
    _ota_safe = safe;
}

void ota_check_and_update() {
    if (!_ota_safe) return;   // refuse to proceed if any threat active
    // ... check manifest, download, reboot
}
```

In the main loop, `ota_set_safe()` is called with `true` only when `zone == ZONE_GREEN` (no obstacle within 100 cm) and `motion == MOTION_STATIONARY` (wheelchair not moving). In the moving-wheelchair branch, `ota_set_safe(zone == ZONE_GREEN)` ensures the gate closes immediately when a YELLOW or RED event is detected. This double-gate means an OTA reboot can only occur when the wheelchair is stationary and no obstacle is in the detection zone.

### 6.2 Limitations

The current CAREC implementation has several known limitations. First, the bounding-box distance heuristic assumes a reference object width that may not match all obstacles — very thin objects (e.g., table legs at oblique angles) may be underestimated in apparent size, producing overestimated distance readings. Second, the system is optimised for indoor environments; performance in bright outdoor sunlight has not been validated. Third, the forward-facing camera provides no coverage for rear or side obstacles; this is a planned Phase 2 extension using a Grove IIC ultrasonic sensor. Finally, the optical-flow motion gate may misclassify slow stationary drift as motion in environments with strong ambient optical flow sources (e.g., ceiling fan rotation).
